# Franchise Fatigue

The measurable decline in audience enthusiasm for sequel, remake, and franchise-extension films — even as Hollywood increasingly relies on them — resulting in falling box office returns on established IP.

---

## Explanation

Franchise fatigue describes the paradox at the heart of modern Hollywood: studios double down on sequels and remakes because they appear "safe," but audiences are increasingly less interested in them. The more sequels that dominate the release slate, the fewer originals get made, which makes it harder to prove originals can succeed — a self-reinforcing trap.

---

## The Data

- **2024:** For the first time in 50 years, all top 10 highest-grossing films were sequels
- **2025 US domestic box office:** ~$8.7 billion — down **20%+** from the 2019 peak of $11.4 billion
- Wide releases (2,000+ locations): **94 in 2024**, down 20% from 120 in 2019
- Sequels in wide release: 26 (2024) → 34 (2025) — more sequels, less total revenue

**Specific underperformance:**

| Film | Revenue | Drop |
|------|---------|------|
| Avatar: The Way of Water (2022) | $688.8M domestic | — |
| Avatar: Fire and Ash (2024) | $378.5M domestic | **-45%** |
| Wicked (2024) | $475M domestic | — |
| Wicked: For Good (2025) | ~$350M domestic | **-26%** |

## What Analysts Are Saying

**Alicia Reese**, SVP Equity Research, Wedbush Securities:
> *"The reliance on franchises has been a little trickier the last few years. It's never going to be a home run from here on out, because people are pickier than they used to be."*

**Jeff Bock**, Senior Box Office Analyst, Exhibitor Relations:
> *"Sometimes an industry has to take two steps backwards before going forward again."*

**74% of Gen Z and Millennials** prefer original content over franchises and remakes (Tubi, 2024).

---

## Why It Matters

Franchise fatigue, alongside [[ai-slop]] saturation, is creating the conditions for the [[immersive-entertainment]] shift. Audiences aren't abandoning entertainment — they're abandoning *formulaic* entertainment. The demand for novelty, authenticity, and genuine surprise is rising, not falling. That demand is being met not by better sequels but by entirely new formats.
